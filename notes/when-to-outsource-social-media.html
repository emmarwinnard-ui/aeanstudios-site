When Should a Business Outsource Its Social Media?

The practical signs it's time, the signs it isn't yet, and what to have ready before that first conversation.

By Emma Winnard · Founder, AEAN Studios · Updated September 2026

The clearest sign it's time to outsource is time, not size. If posting has gone irregular because whoever handles it is also running the business, or the account goes quiet for stretches longer than a couple of weeks, that's usually the point where paying someone else starts to pay for itself. Business size and follower count matter far less than most people assume.

This question gets asked the wrong way round a lot. Business owners tend to look for a size threshold, "we'll outsource once we hit X customers or X revenue," when the more useful question is what's actually happening to the account right now, and what it's costing to keep doing it in-house.

Signs it's time

  • Posting has become inconsistent, not from lack of ideas but from lack of time. Gaps of two, three, four weeks between posts are common once whoever runs the account gets busy with everything else the business needs.
  • The account is being run reactively. Content gets thrown together the morning it's needed rather than planned, which tends to show in the quality and the message.
  • Nobody is looking at what's working. Posting continues, but nobody has time to check engagement, reply to messages promptly, or adjust based on what's actually landing.
  • The owner is the bottleneck. If social media only happens when the founder personally sits down to do it, growth in every other part of the business is capped by how much time that one person has left over.
  • Competitors are visibly more consistent. Not necessarily better, just more present, which compounds over months in a way that's hard to catch up on quickly.

Signs it's not time yet

  • There's no clarity yet on who the business is for. A very new business that hasn't settled its positioning is often better spending a few weeks defining that before paying someone else to guess at it.
  • The budget genuinely isn't there. A social media provider working with an unrealistic budget tends to produce unrealistic results. It's reasonable to wait and save toward a proper engagement rather than commit to something that's underfunded from the start.
  • Posting is already consistent and working. If the current approach, even if it's just the owner posting twice a week, is genuinely landing and there's no capacity strain, there's no urgency to change it.

What to have ready before the first conversation

Whoever takes this on, whether that's AEAN or someone else, the first weeks go far more smoothly if a few things exist before the conversation starts:

  • Admin access or logins to the relevant platforms
  • Any existing brand guidelines, logo files, or colour references, even informal ones
  • A rough sense of who the customer actually is
  • A handful of accounts, in any industry, whose tone or visual style feels like a useful reference point
  • An honest answer to what's not working currently, since that's usually more useful than a list of what's wanted

None of this needs to be polished. It just needs to exist, so the first weeks of the engagement go into planning and content rather than extracting basic information that should already be on hand.

It doesn't have to be all or nothing

Outsourcing is often talked about as a single decision, hand the whole account over or keep doing everything in-house, but most providers, AEAN included, will split it. Some businesses want content produced for them, the filming, the photography, the design, while someone in-house keeps posting and replying day to day. Others want the reverse: they keep producing their own content but want someone else managing the calendar, scheduling and community side. Common questions

How do I know if my business is ready to outsource social media?

The clearest sign is time, not size. If posting has become irregular because whoever handles it is also doing three other jobs, or the account has gone quiet for stretches longer than a couple of weeks, that's usually the point where outsourcing starts to pay for itself.

Is it too early to outsource social media as a small business?

Not necessarily. What matters more than size is whether there's a basic sense of who the business is for and what it wants social media to do. A very new business without that clarity yet is often better spending a few weeks defining it first.

What should I prepare before hiring a social media manager?

Logins or admin access to the relevant accounts, any brand guidelines or existing content, a rough sense of who the customer is, and a few examples of accounts whose tone or style is a reference point.

Can I outsource just part of social media, like content creation?

Yes. Many providers will handle filming, photography and content creation while the business keeps posting in-house, or the reverse. It doesn't have to be all or nothing.
